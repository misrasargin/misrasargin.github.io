Markdown or waste?

Fresh food that is not sold today is thrown away tomorrow. A discount sells more of it, but for less money. Using product-days from an online grocer, I measured what a discount buys, turned that into an evening markdown rule, and replayed held-out days to see whether the rule pays.

How it works, in plain words

Predict

A product’s sales on a discounted day are divided by what it normally sells on that weekday. Averaged over hundreds of thousands of days, that ratio is the lift.

Decide

For each price, the rule works out how much of the stock would sell before closing, allowing for good and bad evenings, and picks the price with the most revenue.

Measure

The first 60 days build the rule. The last 30 days test it on sales it has never seen.

Stock-outs

A product that ran out shows sales, not demand. of product-days had no stock-out, and only those are used.

What this cannot tell you

  • The discounts were not an experiment. The grocer chose when to discount, often together with a promotion slot, so part of the lift is the promotion and part may be timing.
  • The dataset has sales but no stock counts and no expiry dates. The morning stock and the “expires tonight” rule are my assumptions.
  • The replay applies the measured lift to real evening demand. It tests the rule, not the lift itself, in a live shop.
  • A discount on one product can take sales from its neighbours on the shelf and from tomorrow. This analysis sees one product at a time.
  • Products are anonymous codes and sales are in scaled units, so I cannot say which foods respond most.
