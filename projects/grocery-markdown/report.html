Markdown or waste? Pricing fresh food before it expires

Data

FreshRetailNet-50K, published by Dingdong Fresh under CC BY 4.0: store-product pairs ( stores, fresh products) over days, to . Each row has the day’s sales by hour, the day’s discount, a promotion flag, and which hours the product was out of stock. Sales are multiplied by a constant the publisher does not disclose, and stores, products and categories are codes. Everything here is therefore a ratio.

  • of product-days carry a discount of more than 2%.
  • of product-days have no stock-out between 06:00 and 22:00. Only these are used, because on the others sales are capped by the shelf and understate demand.
  • The first days estimate everything. The last 30 test it.

Part 1: what a discount buys

Each product is its own control. Its baseline is its average full-price sales on in-stock days in the first 60 days (at least 8 such days; pairs qualify), adjusted by the weekday pattern of its category. The lift in a discount band is total sales on discounted days divided by total baseline for the same days. Intervals come from resampling products times.

The held-out column uses the last 30 days with baselines from the first 60. It follows the same curve: a little higher than the estimate in the shallow bands and lower in the deepest one. The last two columns split the first 60 days by the promotion flag. At shallow discounts the promotion adds a little. Deep discounts without a promotion are rare and show much larger lifts; they look like a different kind of event, such as a clearance, and I do not rely on them.

Part 2: the evening rule

At 19:00, of a normal day’s sales are still to come. Suppose the shelf holds s times that amount and the stock is worthless tomorrow. At price p (as a share of full price) demand is multiplied by p−e, and the evening itself can be better or worse than normal. The rule picks

the price p that maximises p × E[ min( s, p−e × ξ ) ]

where ξ is the ratio of actual to expected remaining sales, taken from the first 60 days. The cost of the stock does not appear because it is already spent. At 19:00 the rule keeps full price up to about twice the normal remaining sales, then deepens the discount:

Part 3: the replay

For every held-out full-price, in-stock product-day I take the real hourly sales as demand, give the shop a morning stock, and let sales run until the decision hour. Then the remaining stock is priced by one of three policies, and evening demand is multiplied by p−e.

Decision at 19:00. Waste is the share of morning stock unsold at closing. Revenue is the change against never discounting.

Limitations

  • Not an experiment. The grocer decided when to discount. If it discounts products on days it expects to sell well, the lift is overstated; if it discounts to clear slow stock, understated.
  • Stock and expiry are assumed. The data has neither. The replay’s waste levels depend on the morning stock I chose.
  • The replay uses the model’s own lift. It shows the rule is a good decision if the lift is right. It does not re-test the lift.
  • One product at a time. Discounting one item can take sales from similar items and from the next day.
  • One elasticity for everything. Categories differ, from about 0.5 to 3.5. A shop would use its own.
  • A discount is assumed to work at once and equally in every hour. Discounted days have nearly the same hourly shape, which supports this, but those were all-day discounts.
