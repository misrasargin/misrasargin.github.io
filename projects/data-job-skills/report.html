What do data jobs ask for?

Method report for the interactive page.

Summary

From job posts in monthly Hacker News hiring threads ( to ), mention a data role. Each role has a clear set of skills that travel with it: SQL, dashboards and dbt for analysts; Airflow, Spark and warehouses for engineers; statistics and pandas for scientists; PyTorch and fine-tuning for ML engineers. Over three years the largest change in the whole dataset is the mention of AI agents, from to of posts.

Data

“Ask HN: Who is hiring?” is a monthly thread where each top-level comment is one company’s job post. The threads are public and available through the Hacker News search API. I kept the post text only, without author names, and dropped comments under 200 characters, which are questions and replies. The repository stores counts, not text.

A post can mention several roles. Across all posts, mention a junior, intern or new-graduate opening, mention a senior title, and mention remote work.

The pipeline

Four scripts, each with one job:

  • download.py fetches the threads and caches them.
  • build_db.py cleans the HTML and searches each post for 4 roles and skills with the patterns in vocab.py, then writes three tables to SQLite: posts, post_roles and post_skills.
  • queries.sql holds every question asked of the database.
  • analyze.py runs the queries and adds intervals and tests.

For example, the count behind “which skills go with which role” is one join:

SELECT r.role, s.skill, COUNT(*) AS posts
FROM post_roles r JOIN post_skills s USING (post_id)
GROUP BY r.role, s.skill;

Checking the patterns

Keyword matching fails in two ways: it misses wordings and it catches the wrong ones. For the ambiguous skills I read random matches and changed the patterns:

  • R. A bare “R” matched “R&D”. The pattern now needs list context around it. Only posts are left, so R gets no chart.
  • Spark. “Spark Capital” is an investor, not the engine. Excluded.
  • Statistics. “Regression” alone mostly meant regression testing. The pattern now needs “linear regression”, “regression model” and similar.
  • Optimisation. “Problem solvers” matched “solvers”. Removed.
  • Excel and spreadsheets. Almost always described the customer’s pain (“still stuck in Excel”), not a job skill. Dropped as a skill.
  • AI agents. Singular “agent” also means software agents, so only “agents”, “agentic” and “AI agent” style wordings count. Some sales and support “agents” still slip in.

Statistics

Shares come with 95% Wilson intervals, which behave well for small counts.

What is typical of a role. For each role and skill I compare the skill’s share in posts that mention the role with its share in all other posts (a test of two proportions). With four roles and dozens of skills that is many tests, so p-values are corrected with Benjamini-Hochberg at a 5% false discovery rate. A skill is called typical when it passes, appears in at least of the role’s posts, and is at least twice as common with the role as without it.

Limitations

  • The source is one community. It leans to start-ups, software products and senior hires, and to the United States and remote work. It is not the data job market.
  • Posts list several jobs, and skills cannot be assigned to one of them. Ranking by how much more common a skill is with a role reduces this problem but does not remove it.
  • Mentions are not requirements, and a company describing its product (“we build AI agents”) counts like a skill it hires for.
  • The role and skill lists are mine. A skill I did not think of is invisible.
  • Roles with a few hundred posts give wide intervals for rare skills. The charts show them.

Reproduce it

pip install -r requirements.txt, then python analysis/download.py, python analysis/build_db.py and python analysis/analyze.py.
