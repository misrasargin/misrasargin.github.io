Discount or truck? Rebalancing a bike-share system

Method report for the interactive page.

Summary

Bike-share stations drift out of balance during the day. I built the three parts an operator would need to act on that: a forecast of each station’s hourly flow, a rule that turns the forecast into discount offers, and a replay of real rides that prices the result against a truck.

On October 2026, a month the model never saw: the forecast error is lower than a 4-week-average rule. If nobody moved bikes, of rides would fail. Discounts accepted by 30% of riders avoid of those failures, one truck avoids . A discount is the cheaper tool only if it costs less than of the truck’s cost per bike moved.

  • Stations. The system’s public station feed, which gives each station’s position and number of docks.
  • Weather. Hourly temperature, rain and wind from the Open-Meteo archive, converted from GMT to local time.

Part 1: the forecast

For each station and hour I forecast two counts, departures and arrivals, with gradient-boosted trees and a Poisson loss (scikit-learn’s HistGradientBoostingRegressor). Net flow is arrivals minus departures.

Inputs: the station, hour of day, weekday, public holidays, number of docks, weather for the hour, and the station’s own recent history: the last two hours, the same hour yesterday and last week, the 4-week average for this hour, and the total over the last 24 hours. Every input is known one hour ahead, except the weather, where I use what actually happened.

The split follows time. Training uses 29 April to 31 August, September is kept for tuning the discount rule, and every result below is from October.

Two things stand out. The gain over the 4-week average is real but modest, which is normal for hourly counts this small. And weather helps the departure and arrival forecasts but not the net flow: on the rainy hours of October the net error is with weather and without. Rain removes arrivals and departures together.

From a forecast to a probability

The decision needs a risk, not an average. If arrivals and departures are independent Poisson counts, their difference follows a Skellam distribution, which gives the probability of any gain or loss.

P(station runs empty this hour) = P(bikes now + arrivals − departures ≤ 0)

I checked these probabilities against October. For “loses 3 or more bikes” the Brier score is against for always quoting the base rate; for “gains 3 or more” it is against . The calibration chart on the interactive page shows the forecast slightly overstates losses and slightly understates gains in the middle range.

Part 2: the decision rule

At the start of each hour, a station is flagged “needs bikes” if its chance of running empty passes a threshold, and “needs docks” if its chance of filling up does. Then:

  • A rider about to return a bike within m of a station that needs bikes is offered a discount to return it there.
  • A rider about to take a bike within m of a station that needs docks is offered a discount to take it from there.
  • A fixed share of riders accept. I show 10%, 20%, 30% and 50%.

Part 3: the replay

I replay every October ride in time order against simulated dock counts. A rider who finds no bike gives up. A rider who finds no free dock rides to the nearest station with space. Both count as a failed ride. Every station starts each day at 04:00 half full ( bikes in docks). The truck works 06:00 to 22:00 and moves bikes from the fullest stations to flagged ones, instantly. Discount results are averages over random draws of which riders accept.

Does a better forecast give a better decision?

Forecast behind the discount ruleFailed ridesDiscounts paid

Only a little. The model saves a few failures and about 8% of the discounts compared with the 4-week average. Even a perfect forecast changes the result by a small amount. What limits the discounts is their reach: of failures come in the morning peak at stations where no alternative within walking distance has room.

Limitations

  • The trip records contain no dock counts, so the starting state of each day is an assumption. A different starting fill changes the failure counts.
  • The rides happened in a system that was already being rebalanced, and lost demand is not recorded. The replay understates demand at stations that were often empty.
  • Acceptance is a fixed share. Real riders respond to the size of the discount and the extra walk, which I do not model.
  • Truck moves are instant and limited only by a bikes-per-hour budget. Real trucks are slower, so the truck’s advantage is overstated.
  • The Skellam step assumes arrivals and departures are independent within the hour and looks only one hour ahead.
  • The model uses observed weather. A live system would use a forecast, which matters little here because weather barely moves net flow.
  • Trips that start in New York and end in Jersey City are not in these files, so a few arrivals are missing.

Reproduce it

pip install -r requirements.txt, then run, in order, analysis/download.py, prepare.py, forecast.py, simulate.py and build_results.py. Random seeds are fixed. The whole run takes about four minutes on a laptop.
