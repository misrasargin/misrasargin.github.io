Half-life of fame: how fast attention fades

Method report for the interactive page.

Summary

Among the most-read English Wikipedia articles of 2025, had a clear spike. The typical spike loses half of its extra readers in days and is back near its normal level after days. No single curve describes the fade: a power law (steep drop, long tail) fits better than an exponential in of cases. What differs most is the kind of article: a sports event is forgotten within a week, a television series stays elevated for two months.

Data

The Wikimedia Pageviews API reports, for each month, the 1,000 most-viewed articles. I took the first 100 real articles of each month of 2025 (no main page, no special pages), distinct articles in total, and downloaded their daily views by human readers from for 18 months. Each article also gets its one-line English description from Wikidata. Both sources are public domain.

Finding the spike

  • The peak is the article’s biggest day in 2025. The window before and after it is always inside the data.
  • The normal level is the median of days 60 to 8 before the peak. The last week is left out because many events build up.
  • A spike counts if the peak is at least times the normal level and at least views.

On days 1 to I fit a straight line to the logarithm of extra views against the day (an exponential) and against the logarithm of the day (a power law), and compare how much variance each explains.

Limitations

  • Page views are a measure of curiosity on one website in one language.
  • Daily data cannot resolve a fade faster than a day. Many half-lives are below one day and are interpolated.
  • The sample is articles that reached a monthly top 100, so it describes big spikes only.
  • Classification by description is rough. Misfiled articles blur the differences between kinds.
  • Follow-up events inside the 90 days lengthen the tail and are not removed.
  • Comparing R² of two straight-line fits is a simple shape check, not a formal test between the two models.
